## Overview

A FormWizard is a sequence of steps where each one gates the way forward and none of
them gates the way back. It is a Client Component.

**It is not `Steps`, and the difference is a check rather than a drawing.** `Steps` is
an ordered indicator of where a reader is: it derives three marks from one number, it
marks the current step with `aria-current`, and it throws when the marks disagree,
which is a stronger guarantee than any arrangement drawn here. It has no opinion at
all about whether a reader may move, because showing a reader their position and
deciding whether they may leave it are different jobs, and upstream ships the second
as the first with the decision left out. This Component composes `Steps` for the rail
and owns the gate. A caller who wants a rail composes `Steps`; a caller who reaches
for `Tabs` has picked the wrong Component, because a tab list is peer views of one
subject and a wizard's steps are a route through it.

**Back is always allowed, and that asymmetry is the whole Component.** Forward is
gated, and the gate is the point: a wizard exists to stop a reader submitting a
sequence with a hole in it. Back is not, for a reason that is about the reader rather
than about the data. A gated back control traps a reader in the very step they opened
the wizard to escape: they have opened step two, found it asks for something they do
not have, and pressing back does nothing either. No arrangement of the data makes that
acceptable and the fix is not a better error message, it is that going back is always
available. So the back control is drawn, named and operable at every step including
the first, where it is inert rather than absent: a control that appears and disappears
moves the row a reader is aiming at, and its absence on step one would be a claim
that there is nothing before here, which is true and not worth a layout shift to say.

**A refused forward move takes focus to the refusal and announces nothing else.** The
same reasoning as `FormDialog`, and the two are kept consistent on purpose. The
refusal is announced once, by a focus target the reader lands on and can read a list
out from, rather than by an alert and a focus move, which is one refusal heard twice
in two voices. There is no live region on this Component at all, and that is the
decision: a permanently mounted one would announce the refusal a second time when the
focus move commits.

**Forward is gated twice over and the order matters.** The step's own `validate` runs
first, because it is about the reader's answers. The sequence's own `validate` runs
second, because it is about the submission. A step that fails its own rules never
reaches the sequence's, which is what stops a reader being told their card was
declined when the field they left empty was the problem.

**The gate is yours and the drawing is Prism's.** Each step's `validate` decides
whether its step may be left forwards, and this Component refuses the move and draws
what came back. It never marks a control invalid and never shows a message under one,
because it cannot see your form. Your fields are your own `Field`s and your own
`Input`s, and reaching into them would be the override path this package refuses
everywhere else. The cost is that you have to render each message under its control as
well as in the refusal list, and a caller who does only the first has a list a reader
can follow and controls that do not say why.

**The last step's control is a different prop and not a computed label.** `nextLabel`
and `finishLabel` are two claims about what the press does. A sequence whose last
control says "Next" has not told the reader it is the end, and one that says "Finish"
and then opens step four has lied about something a reader will act on. Both are
required, and neither has a default, because this package does not know whether the
product's word is "Continue", "Weiter" or "Siguiente".

## Usage

```tsx
import {
  FormWizard,
  type FormWizardIssue,
  type FormWizardStep,
} from '@nanisoft/prism-ui/components/form-wizard'

const [at, setAt] = useState(0)
const [name, setName] = useState('')

const steps: FormWizardStep[] = [
  {
    id: 'organisation',
    label: 'Organisation',
    description: 'What is this workspace called?',
    validate: (form) => {
      const value = String(new FormData(form).get('name') ?? '').trim()
      return value === ''
        ? [{ field: 'name', message: 'Enter a name for the workspace.' }]
        : []
    },
    children: (
      <Field>
        <FieldLabel htmlFor="name">Workspace name</FieldLabel>
        <Input id="name" name="name" value={name} onChange={onName} />
      </Field>
    ),
  },
  {
    id: 'region',
    label: 'Region',
    description: 'Where is its data held?',
    children: <RegionField />,
  },
]

<FormWizard
  steps={steps}
  current={at}
  onStepChange={(next, direction) => {
    setAt(next)
    if (direction === 'forward' && next === steps.length - 1) submit()
  }}
  validate={() => (terms ? [] : [{ field: 'terms', message: 'Accept the terms.' }])}
  backLabel="Back"
  nextLabel="Continue"
  finishLabel="Create workspace"
  blockedLabel="This step is not finished"
  progressLabel="Workspace setup"
  issueLabel={(field) => (field === 'name' ? 'Workspace name' : 'Terms')}
  instructionsLabel="Continue moves forward and is checked before it goes. Back is always available, including on the first step, where it does nothing."
/>
```

## Guidelines

### When to use

Use a FormWizard for a task with several dependent stages where the order is a route
rather than a preference: setting up a workspace, provisioning an account,
onboarding a member. The gate is what earns it. Without a gate, a multi-step form is
a `Dialog` with a progress bar and no argument for being one screen tall.

Give a step its own `validate` only when it has something to refuse. A step that asks
for nothing has nothing to check, and a `validate` that returns an empty array is a
branch the caller maintains for no reason.

Use the sequence-level `validate` for the checks no single step can make: terms
accepted three screens ago, a plan that was changed under the wizard, a quota that ran
out while the reader was filling in step one.

### When not to use

Do not use it for peer views of one subject. Those are `Tabs`, and a wizard whose
steps may be visited in any order has taught the reader the wrong thing about the
order.

Do not use it when the steps are all on one screen. A two-field form split across two
steps with a rail is a form with a rail.

Do not use it to make a reader agree to something on the last step. A consent that is
gated behind three screens of setup is a consent that is refused, and the right answer
is to ask earlier.

Do not use it for a read-only progress display. That is `Steps`, which is what this
Component composes for its own rail.

### Best practices

- Clamp nothing yourself. `current` is clamped on the way out, so an index that
  outlives a shorter `steps` array lands on the last step rather than on nothing.
- Read `direction` in `onStepChange`. The last step's control submits and does not
  advance, so the index is unchanged and the direction is the only way to tell the
  submission from a step.
- Keep the step objects' `id`s stable. They are what your own per-step state is keyed
  on, and a wizard whose saved answers have nowhere to live is a wizard the reader
  retypes on every visit.
- Give each control an `id` that matches the `field` on its issue. The refusal's
  entries are anchors to those ids.
- Render each message under its control as well as in the refusal list, and set
  `aria-invalid` yourself. The Component cannot see your fields, so it cannot mark
  them.
- Write `instructionsLabel` to say that going back is always allowed. It is the one
  sentence a stuck reader most needs, and it is read once for the whole sequence.
- Use `className` for width and placement only. It cannot change a Prism-owned visual
  property.

### Accessibility

The rail is `Steps` with the caller's accessible name, and it reads the same index
the navigation moves, so the two cannot disagree about which step is current. The
step's own region is a named `group` labelled by the step's own heading, so the name a
reader hears and the name a sighted reader reads are the same text by construction.

The refusal is a named `group` with `tabIndex={-1}`: a script focus target and not a
tab stop, because it exists only after a refusal and a region that appears and
disappears must not leave a permanent hole in the tab order. Focus moves once per
refusal and not on every render, so a reader who tabs away and comes back is not
dragged onto it again.

The back control is `aria-disabled` on the first step rather than natively disabled:
it keeps its place in the tab order and its ring, and the refusal is in the fact that
there is nothing before the first step. A reader paging a wizard is pressing at the
ends to find out whether there is more.

The forward control is the form's submit, so `Enter` in any control inside the step
moves forward. That is what a reader expects from a form that shows one step at a time,
and it is why the handler is on the form rather than on a click.

### Content guidelines

- Write each step's `label` as a noun a reader would use about it: "Organisation",
  "Region", "Retention".
- Write each step's `description` as one sentence saying what this step asks for, in
  the imperative or as a question. It is read on arrival, not scanned.
- Write `progressLabel` as the sequence's noun: "Workspace setup", "Account
  provisioning".
- Write `nextLabel` and `finishLabel` as two different claims, and make sure the last
  one really is the last.
- Write `blockedLabel` with the count where you can: "Two fields need attention".
- Write `issueLabel` as the field's own name, the one on its label, so the refusal and
  the form say the same thing.
- Write each issue's `message` as what to do rather than what is wrong.

## Motion

There is no motion here beyond what `Steps` and `Button` already draw, and that is
deliberate rather than an omission. A refused forward move is a state change on the
step's own region, not a transition the reader is waiting to watch, and a panel that
slides while a reader is being told their field was empty moves the thing they are
about to read. The rail's marks change on the same state feedback every other state
change in this package uses.
