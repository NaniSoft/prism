## Overview

Reveal is a section that meets the reader: the wrapper that binds an arrival to
the reader's own scroll position and to nothing else. It is a style the browser
resolves rather than a state a script flips, so there is no observer, no
listener and no client boundary, and the content exists from the first paint.

## Usage

```tsx
import { Reveal } from '@nanisoft/prism-ui/components/reveal'

<Reveal>
  <FeatureGrid01 columns={…} cells={…} />
</Reveal>

<Reveal delay={1}>
  <ProductGrid01 products={…} />
</Reveal>
```

## Guidelines

### When to use

Use it on the bands a reader scrolls to. An arrival acknowledges the reader's
own movement, which is the only input it answers to, and a band that enters
the viewport as the reader arrives reads as a page composed for reading rather
than as one long poster.

Stagger with `delay` when bands arrive in a rhythm, and keep the stagger to a
page's own measure: three steps exist, and a cascade longer than the rhythm it
sits in is a wait the reader did not cause.

### When not to use

Do not wrap the hero. A section above the fold when the page loads has already
finished entering, and the page does not introduce itself twice; the one
authored moment a page carries belongs to its hero, not to every band under
it.

Do not wrap everything. A reveal on every section is one identical entrance
wearing four different clothes, and the second arrival a reader notices is the
one where the device stops being about the content. Let the still bands
between the arriving ones carry their own weight.

Do not use it for feedback or for state. A control answering a reader is the
first law of motion and already has its scale; an arrival is the third law and
holds exactly one job.

### Best practices

- Compose it around a Block, not inside one. The wrapper is the whole of the
  component, every Block keeps its own surface, and no Block widens for a
  behaviour the whole page shares.
- Remember the fallbacks are the design: a browser without scroll-driven
  animations shows the section simply there, a reader who asked for less
  motion gets the same still page, and neither of those outcomes is a failure
  to be worked around.
- The `delay` steps are fractions of the entry rather than waits, so a reader
  who scrolls fast arrives fast. That is the property that separates this law
  from a timer, and it is worth keeping.
