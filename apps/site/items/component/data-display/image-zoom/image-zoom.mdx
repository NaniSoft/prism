## Overview

An ImageZoom magnifies an image the reader is already looking at, where it sits.
It is one export, `ImageZoom`, and it draws four things in the order a reader
meets them: the image inside a frame that scrolls once the image outgrows the
frame, three controls that magnify, reduce and fit, a polite announcement of the
magnification in force, and a `controls` slot for everything else a reader might
want beside a magnifier.

The four required props are `src`, `alt`, `label` and `scale`, and the three
required names are `zoomInLabel`, `zoomOutLabel` and `resetLabel`. The range is
three numbers with defaults: `minScale` is `1`, `maxScale` is `4` and `step` is
`0.5`.

**It is the `Lightbox` with the dialog taken out, and that subtraction is the
whole Component.** A `Lightbox` opens a portal, traps focus, locks page scroll,
swallows the Escape key and hands the reader a different place in the document to
come back from. All five of those are the right price for an image the page has
not shown the reader at all, where the enlargement is the only way to see it.
They are the wrong price for an image the reader is already looking at: paying a
focus trap to make a picture on the page bigger costs the reader their scroll
position, their place in the document and their focus context, and a reader who
has magnified an inline diagram and then closed the dialog lands somewhere they
have to find again. So there is no portal, no overlay, no trap, no Escape key
and no dismiss here. The page does not move and nothing is taken away from it.
**Use a `Lightbox` when the enlargement is the only route to the image; use an
ImageZoom when the image is already on the page and only its size is in the way.**

**Prism draws the three controls and asks for their names, rather than taking the
controls as a slot.** A slot would move the one guarantee that makes a magnifier
usable to the caller: that the controls exist, that they are real buttons in the
tab order, that they are disabled at the ends of the range, and that a reader
whose control has just been disabled is moved to one that still works. That last
one is not obvious and `Carousel` had to write a `useEffect` to get it right,
because a disabled button leaves the tab order without giving up focus: a reader
who presses magnify at the ceiling is left focused on a control that can no
longer answer Enter, Space or anything else, stranded inside the figure with no
working control under their fingers. This Component does that move itself. **The
cost is that a caller who wants their own magnification controls cannot suppress
these three**, and the answer is that this is the wrong Component for a caller
who wants that.

The magnification is a transform rather than a size, and panning is a scroll
rather than a gesture. Both are stated under Motion and Panning below, and the
first of them carries a price this Component names rather than hides: the frame
reaches its final scroll extent before the pixels arrive.

## Usage

```tsx
import { ImageZoom } from '@nanisoft/prism-ui/components/image-zoom'

<ImageZoom
  src={topology.src}
  alt="Four sources feeding one pipeline, with the webhook source marked as retrying"
  label="Collector topology, west region"
  scale={(percent) => `${percent} percent`}
  minScale={1}
  maxScale={6}
  step={0.5}
  zoomInLabel="Magnify the diagram"
  zoomOutLabel="Reduce the diagram"
  resetLabel="Fit the diagram to its frame"
/>
```

## Guidelines

### When to use

Use an ImageZoom for a figure that is on the page and is legible in outline but
not in detail: a diagram at thumbnail width, a chart exported as a picture, a
screenshot of a console, a photograph of a receipt, a logo lockup with a wordmark
in it. The reader has the page in front of them and wants to look closer without
leaving it.

Use it where the page continues. An inline diagram a reader has magnified and
scrolled is still in the paragraph that introduced it, with the paragraph above
it and the one below it where they left them.

Use `controls` for everything beside the magnifier that is not magnification, and
hand off to a `Lightbox` when the reader wants the whole page rather than this
figure larger:

```tsx
<ImageZoom
  src={topology.src}
  alt={topology.alt}
  label="Collector topology, west region"
  scale={(percent) => `${percent} percent`}
  zoomInLabel="Magnify the diagram"
  zoomOutLabel="Reduce the diagram"
  resetLabel="Fit the diagram to its frame"
  controls={<Button variant="outline">Show the whole page</Button>}
/>
```

### When not to use

Do not use it as the only route to the image's detail. A reader with a keyboard,
a screen reader, or a browser that never loads the picture must still be able to
reach the information. A magnified picture is an aid, not a document.

Do not use it for an image the reader can already read where it sits. The controls
cost a row of chrome and a live region for a picture nobody will magnify.

Do not use it to enlarge an image the page has not shown yet. If the image is not
in the flow, an overlay is the right shape, and that is `Lightbox`.

### Best practices

- Write `alt` for the image, not for the magnification. It is the description the
  reader gets before, during and after, because magnifying changes no semantics:
  the element is still an `img` with that `alt`.
- Write `scale` as a sentence, not a number. It receives a percentage rather than
  the multiplier the Component stores, because a percentage is the form a reader
  reads, and the words around it are the caller's.
- Name each of the three controls for the action it performs, in the caller's own
  words. The two steppers are two different actions and deserve two names rather
  than one word and a state, and the fit control is the one a reader cannot guess
  at, so it wants the clearest of the three.
- Choose a range the picture can survive. `maxScale` past the file's own pixels
  magnifies interpolation rather than detail, and a reader who asks for 800
  percent and gets a blur has been told a lie by the control.
- Expect the range to be substituted when it cannot be drawn. A `minScale` that is
  not a positive number, a `maxScale` that is not above it and a `step` of zero
  are all replaced with values that can hold, and the value actually in force is
  written on the frame as `data-scale`, which is the same thing `AspectRatio`
  does with `data-ratio` so a substituted value is legible in the markup rather
  than only in the screenshot.
- Expect two controls at the fitted size and three above it. The fit control is
  drawn only while the image is magnified, because a reader who has moved away
  from the fitted size needs one press back to it, and it is absent rather than
  disabled at the floor for the reason `Carousel` omits controls that move
  nowhere. A button that does nothing is a claim that there is somewhere to go.
- Read `onScaleChange` as a notification, not as a controlled prop. The
  magnification is held here and not lifted, so a reader who magnified a diagram,
  went away and came back finds it as they left it rather than as they found it.
  What a caller usually wants the number for is layout, and a notification is
  enough for that.
- Keep `className` to layout. Changing a Prism-owned visual property from here is
  prohibited, and the `controls` slot is the supported way to change what sits
  beside the image.

### Accessibility

The three controls are real buttons, so Tab reaches them and Enter and Space
press them, and each draws its focus ring at full strength. The icons are
`aria-hidden`, because a button named "Magnify the diagram" that also announces a
magnifier is announced twice for no gain.

A control that has just been disabled does not keep the reader's focus. This
Component moves focus to the sibling that is still live, and only when the
control that just went is one of its own two steppers, because a disabled button
leaves the tab order without giving up focus: a reader who magnifies to the
ceiling would otherwise be left focused on a control that can no longer answer
anything, stranded inside the figure with no working control under their fingers.

The current magnification is in an `aria-live="polite"` region, in the caller's own
words. It is polite rather than assertive because the reader caused the change by
pressing a control and does not need to be interrupted by it. The number matters
more than the picture: a reader who cannot tell whether they are at 150 or 400
percent cannot tell whether the detail they are looking for is off the frame or
not there.

The root is `role="group"` with the caller's `label`, not a landmark. A group is
announced where it is met and never appears in a landmark list, so a page with six
figures does not put six new entries in a reader's list of regions to walk. It is
also the only thing that tells a screen reader which of two sets of three
look-alike controls the reader has reached.

### Motion

The scale transition is `motion-safe:transition-transform` on `duration-base` with
`ease-out`, and it is the only motion on the surface. Magnifying a picture is
spatial movement of a whole surface, which DESIGN.md says to guard rather than
shorten, so a reader who has asked for reduced motion gets the new size
immediately and loses nothing but the travel between the two.

The transition is on `transform` and on nothing else. An animated `width` or
`height` is a layout animation on the main thread, which is the defect the
compositor rule in the ambient layer exists to prevent, and a magnified diagram
is large enough for that to be visible.

**The scale reaches its final value before the pixels do, and that is the price of
using a transform.** A transform does not change layout, so the frame scroll
extent is already at its full size on the first frame of the transition and the
scrollbar arrives while the image is still travelling. The alternative is to
animate the image's own size, which is a main-thread layout animation on the
largest element on the page, and a scrollbar that moves a fraction early is a
cheaper defect than a stuttering diagram.

### Panning

Panning is a scroll, not a gesture. Once the image is larger than its frame the
frame scrolls, and the reader pans with the keys they already use: the arrow
keys, the space bar, Home and End, and a trackpad or a finger. There is no drag
to pan, no pinch and no double tap, because each of those is a bespoke pointer
model a reader has to discover and a scrollable frame is a thing every reader
already knows.

The frame is a block rather than a centred row, because a centred child larger
than its scroll container has a left edge that cannot be scrolled back to in more
than one browser, and a magnified image is routinely larger than its frame. It
also contains overscroll, so a reader who has reached the edge of the picture does
not drag the page behind it on the way.

### What this is not

There is no drag to pan, no pinch, no double tap, no rotation, no download, no
full-screen API and no comparison against the un-magnified image.

There is no way to replace the three controls. `controls` is drawn after them and
holds what a caller wants beside a magnifier, so a caller who wants their own
magnification controls has to compose them elsewhere, and the answer to that is
that this is the wrong Component for a caller who wants it.

It is a client Component, because it holds a magnification and answers presses,
and it is a small one: three buttons, a live region and a transform, with no
gesture library and no animation runtime behind them.

### Content guidelines

- Write `alt` as a description of what the image says, not of what it is. "Four
  sources feeding one pipeline, with the webhook source marked as retrying" is an
  alt. "Diagram" is a filename.
- Write `label` for the group as what this particular figure is, so a reader
  passing a page of six knows which controls they have reached.
- Write `scale` so it reads as a measurement rather than a status: "150 percent"
  is a position on a scale, and a reader can reason about how much further is
  left.
