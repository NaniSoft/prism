## Overview

A ProportionList draws each labelled category's share of one whole as a bar, with the
share printed beside it. The unit is a share, which is what shapes the whole
Component: the bars are constrained to sum to the total, so they cannot be modelled as
independent series and there is no `series` prop, no `mark` and no axis.

Two decisions shape it. The bar is drawn with `Meter`, so the bar, its tone vocabulary
and its accessibility belong to one Component rather than two. And the bar is
decorative: a `Meter` announces its name and its value, the row prints both, and an
announced bar beside a printed one is the same sentence twice on every row of the page.

The third decision is the one that costs. A share needs a whole, the whole is a claim
about the caller's data, and Prism knows the sum and not the thing summed. So the
Component prints no total and puts no unit beside the shares, and `caption` is where
the caller writes the sentence that says what the shares are a share of.

## Usage

```tsx
import {
  ProportionList,
  type ProportionListItem,
} from '@nanisoft/prism-ui/components/proportion-list'

const ITEMS: ProportionListItem[] = [
  { label: 'Estate observation', value: 482_400, status: { tone: 'success', label: 'On plan' } },
  { label: 'Market capture', value: 268_900, status: { tone: 'success', label: 'On plan' } },
  { label: 'Agent runners', value: 151_200, status: { tone: 'warning', label: 'Near the cap' } },
  { label: 'Storage overage', value: 18_700, status: { tone: 'destructive', label: 'Over the cap' } },
]

<ProportionList
  label="Where the quarter estate budget went"
  items={ITEMS}
  caption="Of 985,300 credits committed in the quarter"
/>
```

## Guidelines

### When to use

Use a ProportionList when the reader's question is "how much of this is that", asked of
several categories at once. A budget breakdown, a traffic mix, a share of estate
covered, a split of failures by stage. The unit is the share, and the bars being on one
common baseline is what lets a reader compare two of them without reading either
number.

Use it when there are more rows than a donut can hold. Three parts are a `Chart`
`donut`, which answers one glance; eight parts in a ring is a puzzle, and a list of
bars is the shape that survives greyscale and holds up at that length.

Use `total` whenever the list is not exhaustive. A bar measured against the sum of what
is shown overstates every category on the page, and the Component cannot tell an
exhaustive list from a truncated one.

### When not to use

Do not use it for values that are not shares. A series against a shared baseline is a
`Chart` `bar`, and a bounded measurement with a limit on it is a `Meter`. A share needs
a whole and a `Meter` does not.

Do not use it for a table. One measure beside its name is a list; several measures and
a header row is `table`.

Do not use it for a comparison with no whole behind it. "Kiln Wharf collected 42
readings and Harrow Reach collected 19" is two numbers, not two shares, and dividing
them by their sum would answer a question nobody asked.

Do not use it to sort. The order you pass is the order drawn and read, and that is
almost always the ranking you already computed.

### Best practices

- Pass the count and let the Component divide. A percentage in `value` means the whole
  has been computed twice, once by you and once here.
- Pass `text` per row when the printed reading is more than a share. "1,204 captures"
  and "4 of 9 sites" are both ordinary readings, and a Component that assembled either
  would be shipping a currency or a counting format into every consumer's product.
- Pass `status` as a pair. A tone without words is a colour a screen reader cannot read
  and a sighted reader who cannot separate the tones cannot either, so the Component
  will not render one.
- Write `caption` with the whole in it. The cells print bare shares, so the sentence
  underneath is what says what they are a share of.
- Expect a `neutral` or `info` status to draw a neutral bar. `StatusTone` has five
  members and `MeterTone` has four, and there is no informational fill, so the colour
  meaning of those two is carried by the dot beside the label and not by the bar.
- Order the items yourself and pass `precision` to match the precision of the numbers
  behind them. One decimal place is the default because a share is a rounded quantity.

### Accessibility

The rows are a `ul` of `li`, so a reader moving by list item is told how many
categories there are and each row is one stop. Nothing in a category list is tabular
data: there is one measure and it is beside its name.

**The bar is decorative and the printed number is the reading, said plainly.** The
`Meter` is wrapped in an `aria-hidden` box because it announces its name and its value,
the row already prints both, and an announced bar beside a printed one says the same
thing twice for every row. A reader who cannot rank the fills still has every number,
which is the whole reason the number is printed rather than left to the bar.

The state is never carried by colour alone. The tone fills the bar and the dot beside
the label, and the words naming the state are in the row, so a reader who cannot
separate `warning` from `destructive` still reads which is which.

Nothing moves, and nothing is interactive. A hover state on a row that cannot be
pressed is an affordance for something the page does not do.

### What this is not

There is no stacked bar, no total printed on the figure, no interactive sort, no row
selection and no tooltip. A stack puts the shares in one figure where the reader has
to find the boundary between two categories to compare them, and this list exists to
put each one on its own common baseline. The total is absent because Prism knows the
sum and not the thing summed, and a number with no unit beside it is the number a
reader misreads.
