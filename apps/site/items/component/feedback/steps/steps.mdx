## Overview

A Steps rail says where a reader is in a sequence: an import, a checkout, a
migration, a four stage release. The caller passes the steps and the index the
reader is on, and every mark is derived from those two facts, so moving the
reader moves all of them and no combination of the props can say they have
finished the thing they are doing.

`state` exists as a per step override, and it is there for a sequence that is
not the process. A reviewer approved a line out of band, a stage was completed by
hand last night, a reader has already answered the question step two would have
asked. In those cases the position and the state are two different facts, and a
derived mark would draw a claim the data contradicts. The cost is named in the
component's documentation: an override is a second source of truth about a
position, which is why it is one prop on one step rather than a mode.

The rule between the steps is the indicator, and the reason is that the
alternatives restate the same fact. A filled bar under every completed step says
"this one is done" once per step, so a reader is handed the same sentence six
times and has to combine them to learn the one thing they came for. The
connector says it once, and it measures the space between two stages, so it is
the only element that shows the sequence has a length at all.

## Usage

```tsx
import { Steps, type Step } from '@nanisoft/prism-ui/components/steps'

const IMPORT: Step[] = [
  { label: 'Choose a source', description: 'A repository or an upload' },
  { label: 'Map the columns', description: 'Six fields we can read' },
  { label: 'Preview', description: 'Nothing is written yet' },
  { label: 'Write', description: 'One transaction' },
]

<Steps
  label="Import progress"
  steps={IMPORT}
  current={2}
/>
```

## Guidelines

### When to use

Use Steps when a reader is partway through a process with an order they are
following, and knowing where they are saves them from reading the whole list. An
import, a checkout, a migration, a release, a four question form.

Use `description` for the qualifier a reader who has just arrived at that step
wants, and keep `label` to the short noun or verb phrase they are scanning for.
The two answer different questions and have different room.

### When not to use

Do not use Steps for navigation. A list of links the reader chooses between is a
`Nav` or a `Menu`; a rail implies a route through the sequence rather than a set
of places.

Do not use Steps for a task with no sequence. That is `Progress`, and a bar that
reports one value is quieter than four numbered discs and a rule.

Do not use Steps to show history. A rail is read as the present and the future,
not as a log; a run of past events with times is a `Timeline`.

Do not override `state` on every step. A caller who has done that has replaced the
rule with their own and gained nothing over passing `current` alone.

### Best practices

- **Pass `current` and let the states derive.** That is the arrangement that
  cannot disagree with itself. Move `current` and every mark moves with it.
- **Override one step, with a reason.** `state` is for a step that was reached
  out of band, and the reason belongs in that step's `description` so the rail
  itself stays a rail. Overriding a step that is not the current one with
  `'current'` is refused: exactly one step is current and the component throws
  rather than draw two answers to where the reader is.
- **Keep `current` in range.** It is clamped, so a stale value from a state that
  has not caught up lands on the nearest real step rather than on nothing.
- **Name the rail when a page has two.** A checkout and an import both have four
  steps and look identical without their names, so pass `label` whenever a page
  carries more than one rail.
- **Keep labels to a few words.** Six steps in a row share the width, and the
  label that gets truncated is chosen by the container rather than by the reader.

### Accessibility

The rail is an `<ol>`, so a reader moving by list item is told which item this is
and how many there are, and the current one carries `aria-current="step"`. That
attribute is on the current step and nowhere else, because a colour is not a
position in a sequence.

The three states are not announced, and that is deliberate. They are consequences
of the position, so a reader who has been told "item two of six, current" knows
the state of the other five without being told six more words. Announcing them
would also mean shipping the words "complete" and "upcoming" into every
consumer's product, which is the defect the copy gate holds a dialog's close
button for.

The three states also differ by shape before they differ by colour: a filled disc
for a step that is done, an outlined one for the step the reader is on, and a
filled muted one for a step ahead. A reader who cannot separate mint from a mint
outline is being told the same thing about two different steps, and the ordinal
on every disc keeps the sequence countable when the colour is gone.

The rule between the steps is `aria-hidden`. It is a measurement of how far
through the reader is, and the position that measurement describes is already on
the list item.

### Content guidelines

- Write labels as short noun or verb phrases, in the reader's terms. "Map the
  columns", not "Column mapping".
- Write descriptions for the step the reader has just reached, not for the step
  in general. "Nothing is written yet" tells a reader what is safe to do; "This
  step is optional" does not.
- Use past tense for what has happened and an infinitive for what has not, so the
  text agrees with the rail without repeating its state.
- Say why a step was reached out of band in that step's description, so an
  overridden state is never the only record of what happened.
