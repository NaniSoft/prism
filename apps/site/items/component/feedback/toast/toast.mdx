## Overview

A Toast reports a fact the reader would otherwise miss: work finished in the
background, a document was shared with them, a setting took effect. It arrives
on its own, it is announced, and it leaves again.

**It is deliberately not the place for a message the reader must act on.** A
message that requires an action cannot be allowed to expire before the action is
taken, and a toast is a surface whose whole nature is expiring. A toast carrying
a blocking message is a modal in a costume. An error the reader caused belongs in
an `Alert` beside the control where they can see what to change; a message that
blocks belongs in a `Dialog`; a message the reader must act on but that does not
block can be a toast at `duration={0}`, which turns the clock off.

**It never takes focus.** That is the load-bearing accessibility claim. A surface
that moves focus to itself has interrupted the reader, and being interrupted is
the thing a toast exists to avoid: the reader is typing, or reading, or doing
something else entirely. So the root is a `role="status"` region that is
announced without being entered, the dismiss control is reachable by Tab from
wherever the reader already was, and nothing in the Component calls `focus()`.

**It is announced once, and that is why there is no countdown on screen.** A
visible timer would have to be text or an attribute that changes, and anything
that changes inside a live region is something a screen reader may announce
again. A toast that read out its remaining time every second would be read
thirty times. The clock lives in a ref, changes no text, and the region speaks
once on arrival and again only if you change the words.

**It pauses on hover and on focus, and the pause holds the remaining time.** A
reader who has moved the pointer onto a toast is reading it; a reader who has
tabbed into it is about to press something. In both cases the clock is the enemy
of what the reader is doing. The pause *holds* what was left rather than
restarting it, so a reader who hovers for ten seconds and then leaves gets the
four seconds they had left, not four more.

The default `duration` of 4000 is deliberately longer than a toast usually wants
to be. The failure it exists to avoid is a toast vanishing before a screen
reader has finished announcing it, which is worse than no toast at all: the
reader is told something happened and then told, by its absence, that they missed
it. `closeLabel` is required with no default, because a toast arrives on its own
and an icon-only control a reader reaches towards has to say what it is.

**Omitting `onDismiss` makes the toast permanent.** Every way out of the
Component hands over to you, so with nobody to hand to there is no clock to run,
no dismiss control to render and no Escape to answer. The alternative would be a
toast that faded to invisible and was never removed from the document: gone from
the screen, still in the live region, still taking the pointer.

## Usage

```tsx
import { Toast } from '@nanisoft/prism-ui/components/toast'

// The caller unmounts. The Toast never removes itself, because an element that
// disappears immediately has no exit to animate.
const [open, setOpen] = useState(true)

return open ? (
  <Toast
    title="Invoice sent"
    description="Acme Ltd can see it now."
    closeLabel="Dismiss notification"
    onDismiss={() => setOpen(false)}
  />
) : null
```

## Guidelines

### When to use

Use a Toast for work the reader started and did not have to watch: an upload that
finished, a document that was shared, a setting that took effect, a background
job that completed. The reader would miss it entirely if nobody said anything,
which is the test: if they would not miss it, it is not a toast.

Use it for one thing at a time. A toast that arrives for each of four files
uploaded is four interruptions, which is the failure the Component is supposed to
be avoiding. Report the batch as one toast.

Use `duration={0}` for the toast the reader has to do something about, and give
it a `closeLabel` and an `action`. That is the one case where a toast earns the
name, because the reader's next step is the toast's entire purpose.

### When not to use

Do not use a Toast for an error the reader caused. Put it in an `Alert` beside
the control, where the reader can see what to change and fix it. A destructive
tone on a toast is for a failure the reader did not cause and would not
otherwise learn: a payment declined on the server, a session that expired, a
deploy that failed.

Do not use a Toast for anything blocking. That is a `Dialog`, and the difference
is not politeness: a blocking message has to survive the reader ignoring it, and
a toast cannot promise that.

Do not put a required action in a toast and nowhere else. A transient surface is
not a reliable route to anything: the reader may have missed it, or their screen
reader may not have finished announcing it when it went. Whatever the `action`
does has to be reachable in the surface they are already looking at, and the
toast is the shortcut rather than the gate.

Do not use a Toast to confirm a destructive action. "Deleted" arriving as a toast
means the reader cannot undo from the surface they are on, and undo is the whole
reason destructive actions are recoverable.

### Best practices

- Unmount on `onDismiss`. The Toast asks to be taken off the page and never takes
  itself off, because an element that is gone immediately has no exit to
  animate. A caller who expects it to remove itself will write a no-op and leave
  a toast parked on the page forever.
- Always pass `onDismiss`, even for a toast that should not expire. Reach for
  `duration={0}` for that case, not for a missing handler: without a handler the
  toast is permanent, which is almost never what a caller meant to write.
- Keep the title to a few words that are still true, and put the sentence under
  it. A title is scanned and a sentence is read; a toast that merged them forces
  a reader to read a heading.
- Set the tone to match what happened, and remember that `destructive` means the
  reader did not cause it. Every tone sets its own fill and its own ink, because
  a toast is the one surface in this package that is a coloured surface with body
  text sitting on it.
- Do not put a countdown on screen. It changes a live region four times a second.
- Use `paused` rather than a long `duration` when a batch of operations is
  reporting one at a time. It is the same intent and it is reversible.
- Render toasts inside a fixed layer that has `pointer-events-none`, because a
  layer over the whole viewport will otherwise swallow the pointer from the page
  underneath it. The Toast sets `pointer-events-auto` on itself for exactly this
  reason.
- One toast host per corner, and do not stack more than three. A wall of toasts
  is a queue the reader has to read before they can read the page.

### Content guidelines

- Write the title as a completed fact, in the past or present perfect: "Invoice
  sent", "Changes saved", "Copied to clipboard". A title in the present
  continuous is a promise, and a promise that expires is worse than silence.
- Put anything the reader must do in the `action`, not in the words. The words
  are for information; the action is for the request.
- Keep `closeLabel` short and specific: "Dismiss notification", not "Close". It is
  the accessible name of a control a reader reaches for without having opened
  anything, and four products in two languages cannot all be told the control
  says "Close".
- Do not put a sentence in the `title` and a shorter one in the `description`.
  The order is the scan order, and a reader who only catches the title should
  still know what happened.
