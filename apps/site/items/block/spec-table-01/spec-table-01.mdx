## Overview

SpecTable01 is a capability's limits as a real table: what is measured, its value, its
unit and the condition it holds under, grouped into bodies you name, with a summary
band of figures underneath.

The storefront version of this pattern is a product's specification table: a list of
attributes for an item for sale, the material, the weight, the dimensions, the care
instructions, in a two-column table under a heading, sometimes grouped into sections.
Every part of the layout and every part of the accessibility work transfers without a
change, because a specification is a table whichever subject it describes: a screen
reader announces the row's name with every cell beneath it in one, and a grid of `div`s
does not. What does not transfer is the subject. The figures here are a capability's
limits: what it handles, what it costs, what it will not do.

## What this Block will not do

**It will not derive a value, infer a unit, or round a number.** Those three refusals
are why it exists rather than a limitation of it.

A specification table is the one surface in this system where a number a reader cannot
check is a liability rather than a convenience. Every figure on it is something the
reader is about to plan against, and a figure this package printed would be a
measurement the product never made.

| Rejected | Why |
| --- | --- |
| `value: number` | Forces the caller to format outside the Block or lose the precision the figure belongs to |
| A `format` prop | Moves the arithmetic and a locale into a package that has no business choosing either |
| A `compact` or `approximate` boolean | A rounding rule with no name, applied to a figure this package cannot see the meaning of |

So a throughput number is a node you composed, a unit is a string you wrote, and a
precision is your decision made in your own formatter before the value reached the
Block. A rounded retention window is a wrong retention window.

## `unit` is a string beside the value

`unit` is a separate field rather than part of `value`, and the reason is that "12k",
"12 thousand" and "12000" are three renderings of one figure. Which is right is a fact
about the measurement and the audience rather than about the measurement alone: a
reader reconciling a bill wants the full number, a reader scanning a column of twelve
wants the short one, and a machine reading an export wants neither.

The caller owns the rendering in `value` and the precision in `unit`, and the Block
draws them as two cells. The cost is that a caller who wants them joined has to join
them, which is the right place for the decision because it is their figure and their
audience.

## Usage

```tsx
<SpecTable01
  title="What the connector handles"
  columns={[
    { id: 'label', header: 'What is measured' },
    { id: 'value', header: 'Value' },
    { id: 'unit', header: 'Unit' },
    { id: 'note', header: 'Measured under' },
  ]}
  groups={[
    {
      title: 'Collection',
      rows: [
        {
          id: 'points',
          label: 'Points per walk',
          value: '7,000,000',
          note: 'Every configured tag, every 15 minutes, per site',
        },
      ],
    },
  ]}
  summary={[{ label: 'Points per walk, whole estate', value: '7.0M' }]}
  empty="We publish no limits for this connector yet."
/>
```

## Guidelines

### When to use

Use it where a reader is deciding whether a capability can carry their estate, their
market or their run. That is a capability page, a connector's page, or the bottom of a
pricing page where the tiers' ceilings matter more than their prices.

Use `emphasis` on the one row that decides the answer for everybody else. That is the
retention window, the ceiling, or the region a capability cannot reach yet.

### When not to use

Do not use it for three or four figures. That is a `Metric` row or a `FactList`, and a
table with a header row and two rows is a header row and two rows.

Do not use it as the summary. A summary is prose, and a specification is measurements;
mixing them puts the argument in the middle of the numbers.

Do not pass a value you cannot defend. The Block will print it exactly as composed,
which is the whole contract and also the whole risk.

### Best practices

- Compose every value in your own formatter before you pass it, including the
  grouping. That is the decision the Block refuses to make for you.
- Put the condition in `note` on every row that has one. A figure with no condition is
  a claim about every case rather than about the one it was measured in.
- Name your groups. Prism has no opinion about whether the facets of a specification
  are "Collection", "Retention", "Regions" or "Limits", and the set of groups is a fact
  about your capability.
- Use the summary band for the two or three figures the table reduces to, and let
  `Metric` draw them. A band with six figures is a second table.
- Declare a column only when at least one row carries it. An empty column throws, and
  the throw is the point: a column of empty cells is a column every reader scans twice.

### Content guidelines

- Write each row's label as the thing being measured. It is the row header a screen
  reader announces with every cell beneath it, and a label that wraps costs every other
  row its alignment.
- Write each note as the condition, not as a caveat about the caveat. "Ninety fifth
  percentile across eleven months of walks" is a condition. "Approximate" is not.
- Write the unit as the notation, not as the sentence. The value cell carries the
  number you composed; the unit cell carries "levels", "min" or "ms".
- Write `empty` as the sentence that says which of the three empty states this is. A
  specification with nothing in it means the capability publishes no limits, or the
  limits have not loaded, or it is not available in this region.
