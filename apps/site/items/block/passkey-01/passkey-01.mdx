## Overview

Passkey01 is the caller's registered authenticators as a list of names, moments and
transports, a register control whose callback is where the browser's own ceremony runs,
and a remove control on every row. Every string is passed in as a prop, and the Block
ships no sentence of its own anywhere on the page.

The storefront's version of this pattern is a security settings page: a list of the ways
in, a control to add another, and a control beside each row to take one away, whose
purpose is to let a shopper see which second factor is protecting their card. The
composition here is that page, unchanged. What the list is made of is different, and
that difference is the whole of this Block's argument.

## It opens no credential store, it calls no WebAuthn API, and it owns no ceremony

Those three sentences are the Block. A passkey is a private key the platform holds and
this repository never sees, bound to an origin and to a relying party, and it is
created or used inside a ceremony the browser runs: a prompt in the platform's own
chrome, outside this page, with the reader's biometric or their device passphrase behind
it.

A Block that reached for that would be choosing three things for every consumer that
installed it, and all three are decisions about a reader's security rather than about
how a list is drawn:

| Chosen by the Block | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| The origin | A credential is bound to the origin that created it, and one created against the wrong origin is unusable on the site that needs it. |
| The user-verification flag | The difference between a ceremony that asks for a fingerprint and one that only proves the presence of a device. A product whose risk assessment says the weaker flag is right must be able to say so. |
| The relying party identifier | A durable name for this deployment, and a product that deploys in two regions has two of them. |

So `onRegister` is where the ceremony runs, and this file holds the list, the labels and
the states and nothing else. What the reader sees is identical either way. What your
server accepts afterwards is entirely yours, and a reader who registered a passkey the
wrong way finds out at the next sign-in.

There is no credential vocabulary in the type either: no `credentialId`, no public key,
no sign count. A type with those would have implied that the Block can read a
credential, and a design system is not a place to reach for one.

## The remove control is on every row, including the reader's own

Withholding the dangerous control from the row the reader is sitting on is the instinct,
and the instinct is wrong for a reason that has nothing to do with the interface. A
reader who wants to remove the credential they are using is a reader who will find
another way, and what they find is a credential they cannot see on a device they are
not holding. So the control is drawn on every row.

What the Block does not do is confirm, because a bare click that destroys a credential is
not a confirmation. A product that wants one composes its own dialog and calls
`onRemove` from there, or passes its own control. And `removeLabel` is a function for
two reasons: a column of controls all announced as "remove" is a column a screen reader
user cannot act on, and removing the credential on a phone and removing the one on a
laptop are different consequences.

## The transport is your string, because this phone and this laptop are sentences

"hybrid" and "nfc" and "internal" are tokens a caller can pass and this package will not
expand into words of its own. So `transport` is whatever your platform reported, drawn
exactly as passed, and `transportLabel` is there for the caller who holds a token and
wants your product's sentence for it. Pass nothing and the row simply has no third fact.

The two moments are the same arrangement: `created` and `lastUsed` are drawn exactly as
passed, and `dateLabel` is a function for the caller who holds a real timestamp and
wants a localised reading. One function for both moments, because a row that says "three
days ago" beside a raw ISO string reads as two different systems.

`lastUsed` is the moment a reader actually came for. A credential that has never been
used is the row that explains why a sign-in failed on a device the reader expected to
work, and drawing nothing beside it leaves the most useful fact on the screen unstated.

## The honest failure is your sentence

A ceremony can fail because the reader dismissed the platform's prompt, because the
reader has no authenticator at all, because the platform refused to create a
credential, because your relying party identifier is not permitted on this origin,
because the origin is not a secure context, because the device is out of room, because
a third party on the device answered instead of them, or because your server refused the
public key after the platform had already created it. Nine outcomes, and the difference
between a reader who cancelled and a reader whose platform is not permitted is the
difference between "try again" and a support ticket.

So `status` is a prop, there is no `registered` flag and no `removed` flag in the file,
and what the Block does about an outcome is the two rendering decisions: the register
control is disabled while you say the ceremony is running, because a second press
starts a second prompt the reader did not ask for, and the outcome is drawn in a live
region, `assertive` for the refusal, because a ceremony that fails silently looks to a
reader exactly like a ceremony that is still thinking.

## What the Block refuses

| Refused | Why |
| --- | --- |
| A row with no `name` | A date and some controls with nothing to say which credential they belong to, on the one screen whose purpose is telling a reader apart from their own devices. |
| `onRegister` with no `registerLabel` | A nameless button that then opens a system dialog, so a reader who presses it has been told the wrong thing about where they are. |
| `onRemove` with no `removeLabel` | Every control announced by the same word, and a reader told which credential to destroy by nothing. |
| A `dateLabel` with no moment, or a `transportLabel` with no transport | A function composed and then discarded. |

## Usage

```tsx
'use client'

import { useState } from 'react'
import { Passkey01, type Passkey01Status } from '@nanisoft/prism-ui/blocks/passkey-01'

export function AuthenticatorList() {
  const [status, setStatus] = useState<Passkey01Status>()

  return (
    <Passkey01
      eyebrow="Nexus"
      title="What can sign in as you"
      description="Each one is a credential your machine holds. Removing one means that device stops being able to act for you."
      authenticators={listOfAuthenticators}
      onRegister={async () => {
        setStatus({ state: 'registering', message: 'The platform is asking for a fingerprint or a face.' })
        // Your ceremony, your three choices: the origin, the verification flag and
        // the relying party identifier. Nothing in this package chose any of them.
        await navigator.credentials.create({ publicKey: yourOptions })
        setStatus({ state: 'idle', message: 'Registered.' })
      }}
      registerLabel="Register a passkey on this device"
      registering={status?.state === 'registering'}
      onRemove={(id) => confirmThenRemove(id)}
      removeLabel={(one) => `Remove the credential called ${one.name}`}
      status={status}
      empty="No credentials registered on this account yet."
    />
  )
}
```

## Guidelines

### When to use

Use it as a whole account security section, or as the passkey half of one beside your own
sign-in-method list. It is the only place in this package where a reader goes looking
for the credential that is protecting them.

Name every authenticator as the reader named it. A passkey is only identifiable by the
person who registered it, and "the phone in my coat pocket" is a name where "Pixel 8" is
a device. A caller whose only record is a product name passes that, and the cost of
doing so is that two rows in a reader's list will be indistinguishable.

Pass `lastUsed` wherever you have it. It is the moment a reader came for and the one
that explains a sign-in that failed on a device they expected to work.

### When not to use

Do not use it to sign a reader in. Registering a credential and using one are different
moments, and a screen that does both is a screen with two ceremonies in it and no way to
recover from the one that failed.

Do not use it where the authenticators have no names. A list of ids is a list a reader
cannot act on, and acting is the only reason the list is here.

Do not expect it to fetch anything. `authenticators` is a projection of what you already
know, and a Block that fetched it would be a Block that ships a transport.

Do not use it to draw a credential's public key or id. The Block has no vocabulary for
either, on purpose, and a consumer that needs one is reaching for a component that does
not exist here.

### Best practices

- Pass `removeLabel` as a function that names the authenticator. Two controls announced
  identically is a list a screen reader user cannot act on, and the honest sentence
  names the device.
- Confirm before you remove. The Block does not, deliberately, and a bare click that
  destroys a credential is not a confirmation. Compose your own dialog and call
  `onRemove` from there.
- Pass `registering` from the ceremony's own promise and `status` from its result. The
  first is the control and the second is the outcome, and on this screen the request in
  flight might be either.
- Pass a `status` whose state you change, because a reader who pressed register and
  heard nothing will press it again and start a second prompt.
- Pass `className` for grid placement only. The list is a list, and a card the reader
  has to scroll to reach the fourth row of is a list they will not check.

### Content guidelines

- Write the title as what the list is. "What can sign in as you" tells a reader what
  they are looking at; "Security" names a settings page they have to trust.
- Write the description as the consequence of removing one. A reader deciding whether to
  destroy a credential needs to know what that device will no longer be able to do.
- Write each name as the reader's own words for the device, and use a sentence where
  one exists. "The laptop on my desk" beats "MacBook Pro", and it beats both when there
  are two of them.
- Write `registerLabel` as a sentence that says where the prompt will appear. "Register
  a passkey on this device" tells a reader that the next thing is a system dialog;
  "Add" does not, and a reader surprised by a platform prompt does not come back.
- Write `removeLabel` as the action plus the thing. "Remove the credential called the
  laptop on my desk" is a sentence a reader can check against the row they meant.
- Write `empty` as a fact about the account. "No credentials registered on this account
  yet" and "we could not read the list" are different sentences, and the second one is
  the one a reader with two credentials needs to be shown.
