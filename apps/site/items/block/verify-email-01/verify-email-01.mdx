## Overview

VerifyEmail01 is where a person finds out whether a message reached them: what is
being verified, where the verification has got to, your own resend, and the route out
for a reader this screen does not help. Every string is passed in as a prop and the
Block holds no state of its own.

**The five states are five screens and not five messages, and that is the whole
design.**

| State | What it is | What changes on screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `checking` | progress | the reader's job is to wait, so a ring is drawn when you named the state and nothing else is offered |
| `verified` | a destination | the reader's job is to leave, so no resend is drawn |
| `pending` | a wait | the resend is drawn and takes the neutral tone, because nothing has gone wrong |
| `failed` | a retry | the resend is drawn, the tone is destructive, and the outcome interrupts |
| `expired` | a request for a new link | the resend is drawn, the tone is a warning, and the outcome interrupts |

A screen that drew any of these as a sentence in the middle of a form would be asking
a reader to work out what has happened from a sentence, and a reader who cannot tell
whether their address is verified will try again. A second attempt on a verification is
a second attempt on a machine's authorisation, and eventually it is a lockout.

**`neutral` is the tone that asserts least and `pending` is the state that gets it.** A
reader who has just followed a link is in the middle of an action they took and the
honest reading of the next two seconds is that nothing has gone wrong. A destructive
dot on a wait is a claim about the reader's account made by a colour, before any
evidence exists, and the reader who believes it is the one who goes looking for the
problem somewhere else. `expired` takes `warning` rather than `destructive` because the
link died, the address may be perfectly good, and the thing to do about it is ask for
another one.

**There is no code field on this screen, and the reason is one sentence: the link is the
proof, and a field invites a reader to type a code they do not have.** A reader who
arrived without the link has exactly one action available, which is asking for another
one, and a field in front of them invites them to type the six characters they half
remember from a text they did not read. Prisms that render a code field on a link-based
verification are answering a product that sends codes.

## Usage

```tsx
import { useState } from 'react'
import { VerifyEmail01 } from '@nanisoft/prism-ui/blocks/verify-email-01'

export function ConfirmAddress({ result }: { result: VerificationResult }) {
  const [resending, setResending] = useState(false)

  return (
    <VerifyEmail01
      eyebrow="Sign up"
      title="Confirm that address"
      description="This is where you find out whether the message arrived. Nothing else about your account changes until it does."
      target={{
        identifier: result.address,
        identifierLabel: 'Address on the account',
        changeLabel: 'Verify a different address',
        onChange: (identifier) => startOver(identifier),
      }}
      status={{
        state: result.state,
        stateLabel: result.label,
        message: result.message,
      }}
      resendLabel="Send it again"
      resentLabel="Sending it now"
      resending={resending}
      cooldownLabel="You can ask again in about a minute."
      onResend={async () => {
        setResending(true)
        await resendVerification(result.address)
        setResending(false)
      }}
      help={{
        message: 'If this address belongs to somebody else, ask whoever sent the invitation to check the address they used.',
        href: '/support/recovery',
        label: 'Ask for help with this address',
      }}
    />
  )
}
```

## Guidelines

### When to use

Use it at the destination of a verification link, and only there. It is a whole section
because the reader has to be able to read the state without scrolling past anything,
and because a dialog gives them nowhere to go if the state is not one they expected.

Pass `stateLabel` for every state. The fallback is the machine value, which is honest
and rarely wanted: a product whose readers meet the word `pending` is a product that
has not named its own states, and seeing its own key on the screen is a faster way to
find that out than shipping it.

Pass `identifierLabel` too. The address is on the screen either way, and the label is
what a reader compares against their own inbox before they press anything.

### When not to use

Do not use it when your product sends a code rather than a link. A reader with a code
needs a field to put it in, and `OneTimeCode` is the Component for that arrangement.

Do not use it as a general status page. The five states are about one verification, and
the tone each one takes is a claim about that verification alone.

Do not put a code field beside it as a fallback "just in case". Two proofs on one screen
is a screen that asks the reader to work out which one their product meant.

### Best practices

- Use `resending` for both facts: that a resend is in flight and that one has been
  asked for. It is what makes the control swap to `resentLabel` and what turns
  `cooldownLabel` on, and from where the reader is sitting those are the same event.
- Pass `cooldownLabel` in your own words and keep it in step with your own rate limit. A
  Block that guessed a cooldown would be shipping one policy to four products in a
  place no consumer can read it.
- Keep `changeLabel` and `onChange` together or neither. The Block throws on half a
  control.
- Compose `actions` for whatever your reader does next rather than asking for a named
  set of controls here. Open another inbox, sign in, contact an administrator: all three
  are real and all three are your product's.
- Do not render your own announcement beside `status.message`. The sentence is inside
  the live region and drawn once, and a second region holding the same words would read
  the same news twice on the one screen where a reader is waiting for it.

### Content guidelines

- Write each state message as a fact about the verification and the reader's position,
  not as a feeling. "The message has not been opened from that address" beats "Almost
  there".
- Write `checking` in the present tense and in the reader's language, because it is the
  one state where the words are doing the work of a progress bar.
- Write `expired` with the lifetime in it. "Links last thirty minutes" is what makes the
  refusal acceptable, and it is the sentence that stops the reader asking a question.
- Write `failed` as something that might have happened to the link rather than as
  something the reader did wrong. A reader who believes they mistyped will leave the
  screen and ask somebody else instead.
- Write the help message as the reason this screen is not helping and what else exists.
  The link label is what following it does, not the sentence above it.
