## Overview

OfferingCategories01 is a set of kinds of capability: a grid of tiles, each with a
name, a count, a one-line description and a link, or a tab row with your own panel
underneath it.

The storefront version of this pattern is a category strip: a row of departments a shop
sells in, each tile carrying the department's name, a line about it, how many products
are in it, and a link into the department. Every part of the layout and every part of
the accessibility work transfers unchanged, because a tile is a tile and a count is a
count whichever set it counts. What does not transfer is what the categories are. The
kinds here are kinds of capability: capture sources, agents, connectors, tiers. A
reader is not looking for the aisle their shopping falls in, so the count beside a tile
is not a shelf size.

## The grid is the default, and the reason is force

Tabs hide five of six answers behind a control. A reader who arrives looking for their
own kind of capability and does not see it among the visible tiles has to work out
whether it is behind a tab, and a reader who does not work it out is a reader who
leaves.

So the cost of hiding is charged to the caller rather than to the reader. The
arrangement is opt-in, and a caller who passes `variant="tabs"` is making the argument
that their kinds are alternatives a reader chooses between rather than a set a reader
checks membership of.

The rejected default was tabs, which is the shape most category strips reach for first,
because it looks more like an interface and less like a page.

| Passed | Answers visible at once |
| --- | --- |
| Nothing, or `variant="grid"` | All of them |
| `variant="tabs"` | One, until the reader asks for another |

The tabs arrangement also needs three props the grid does not: `tabsLabels`, `content`,
and the two words on the controls that move along the strip. `content` is the caller's
own panel, because the Block knows which kind is showing and nothing at all about what
is in it.

## The count needs a label function

"12 sources" and "12 supported" and "12" are three sentences, and they are three
sentences because a count means different things on different pages: on one it is how
many things exist, on another it is how many regions are covered, on a third it is how
many of them a given plan may use.

A Block that picked one would be picking a meaning, and it would be picking it for
every consumer at once. So `count` is a number and `countLabel` is a function of the
number and the kind's own name, and a count with no label throws.

```
countLabel={(count, category) => `${count} ${category.toLowerCase()} shipped`}
```

The cost of the requirement is that a caller who counts in three places writes one
function. That is the correct outcome: a count that means one thing on one page and
another on the next is a number nobody can act on.

## Usage

```tsx
<OfferingCategories01
  title="Five kinds of capability"
  categories={[
    {
      id: 'agents',
      name: 'Agents',
      description: 'What acts on a finding, and what it may touch when it does.',
      count: 6,
      countLabel: (count) => `${count} agents on the runtime`,
      href: '/capabilities/agents',
      hrefLabel: 'Read about the agents',
    },
  ]}
  empty="No kinds match that."
/>
```

## Guidelines

### When to use

Use it where a reader's first question is "is the thing I need one of these". That is a
capabilities index, a platform overview, or the top of a documentation site whose
subjects are kinds rather than tasks.

Use `variant="tabs"` only where each kind has a real panel behind it and the reader is
expected to pick one and read it. A grid of six panels nobody scrolls is a page that
answers nothing about the other five.

### When not to use

Do not use it for the capabilities themselves. That is `OfferingList01`, and the grid
here is a way of finding the list rather than a substitute for it.

Do not use it where each tile needs a paragraph. A tile is a name, a count and one
line, and at two lines of description the tile stops being a list entry and starts
being a paragraph.

Do not pass a count you cannot source. A count is a claim about your own catalogue and
the sentence around it is the sentence a reader will believe.

### Best practices

- Give every tile a description. The field is required and the run throws without it,
  because a tile with only a name is a navigation menu and a menu answers "where can I
  go" rather than "does this recognise my week".
- Write the count sentence to name what is counted. "12 sources connected" and "12
  supported" are different claims and only one of them is yours to make.
- Pass `tabsLabels` only in the tabs arrangement, and `content` only in it too. Both
  throw in the other arrangement rather than sitting in the markup drawing nothing.
- Put the panel in `content` and let it be your own list, table or grid. A panel drawn
  here would be the Block inventing content for the one place on the page where
  content is most specific.
- Keep the tile's `hrefLabel` as what following the link does, not as the kind's name
  again.

### Content guidelines

- Write the kind's name as the product spells it. It is the card title and the tab
  trigger, and a reader switching tabs is scanning it in one line.
- Write the description as one line about what the kind is. The longer answer belongs
  in the panel the link points at.
- Write the two control words as the directions your interface already uses. They are
  visible words on the buttons rather than accessible names for two arrows, because a
  reader who cannot tell which arrow moves back has to guess.
- Write `empty` as the sentence that tells a reader what happened. An empty category
  list means three different things to three different readers.
