A pricing table is a comparison, and the comparison is not the price. Two plans at
twice the price with half the features is not the expensive plan. So the features are
the content, the price is a line in each card, and the reader's real question is
"which of these is for me", which is answered by the feature list and not by
ordering the cards cheapest first.

## Three plans, not four

Two is a comparison. Three is a comparison with a middle. Four or more is a table,
and a table of plans needs `Table` because the reader is now comparing across a row
rather than down a column. If a fourth plan is genuinely needed, the honest answer is
a table and not four cards side by side, which is where "Enterprise" usually goes and
where it stops being comparable to anything.

## Name the middle plan, do not style it louder

`Pricing01` takes a `featured` flag, and it is the only visual difference between the
three. A `Badge` on the featured plan says which one is recommended, in words, and
that is worth more than the shadow, because a reader who cannot decide is looking for
the recommendation rather than inferring it from emphasis.

A featured plan that is not the one most readers should choose is a lie told in CSS.
Pick it from what the product actually wants to sell.

## The features are a set, so the set is rendered as a set

`Pricing01` draws a tick per included feature, and that is right when every plan has
every feature, because the reader is scanning for presence. When plans differ in
*which* features they include, a column of ticks reads as noise: three empty ticks in
a plan is not information, it is a negative rendered three times.

Differing plans want the shared features as ticks and the differences as text, which
is what a `Table` over plans gives you. The rule is whether the reader is looking for
"is this here" (a tick column) or "how do these differ" (a table). The tick is
`Pricing01`'s own business and not an Item of its own, so it is not in this list.

## The price is a sentence with a period in it

A price is a number, a period, and a cadence. "$24 / month" is two facts; "$24" alone
promises nothing about what happens in a year. `Kbd` is for the shortcut, not for the
cadence, and the common mistake is bolding the cadence to make the number look
smaller, which makes the number harder to compare across cards instead.

## What the button promises

Each card's action is a `Button` and its label is what happens next, not the name of
the plan. "Start free" and "Talk to us" are decisions; "Choose" and "Select" are
forms to fill in. The plan that needs a conversation should say so on its own card,
because a reader who has to infer it from a different button style has been asked to
do the sales work.
