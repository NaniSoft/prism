---
title: Patterns
description: "Compositions worth copying: which Items to arrange, and why that arrangement."
---

A Pattern is a document that names the Items it composes. It is not a Block and it is
not a Component: nothing here ships, and nothing here is imported. What is here is
the knowledge of *which* Items to arrange and *why*, which outlives any one
arrangement and belongs to whoever is building rather than to whoever shipped a
component.

Each Pattern declares its composition in its frontmatter, and the build checks those
declarations against the catalogue. A Pattern naming an Item that does not exist
fails the build, because nothing about a prose page is type-checked and a recipe
that names a renamed Item reads perfectly.

## What a Pattern is for

A Block is a decision already made: a hero, a pricing table, an empty state. You
import it and pass it content. It is right for the arrangement its author chose, and
choosing a different arrangement means composing the Items yourself.

A Pattern is for the case where the arrangement is the thing you needed to know. The
Items already exist and are already published; what nobody wrote down is which six of
them go together, in what order, and which one carries the state that is not the
data. That is what a Pattern records, along with the decisions that are easy to get
wrong and expensive to discover late.

The line between the two is whether you are installing something or reading
something. `Pricing01` is a Block. Why three plans and not four is a Pattern.

## What a Pattern is not

It is not a template. There is no scaffold to copy, no file to start from, and no
code that will drift from this page. A Pattern that wanted to be a template would
have to be maintained against the library, and a document that can be wrong in the
same way code can be wrong is a worse thing than no document.

It is also not a rule. Nothing here is enforced on a consumer. The one thing that
*is* enforced is that the Items named here exist, and that is enforced because a
broken reference in a document is invisible rather than loud.

## Templates are the other half

A **Template** declares the **Pages** it arranges, and it is the same kind of
document making the other claim. Where a Pattern is about how to compose a surface,
a Template is about which screens a product has and what order a reader meets them
in. The declaration is the published `DocsNavEntry` union, which is the shape
`DocsShell` already takes.

## The Patterns and the Templates

Each one opens with what the surface is for, then the decisions inside it, including
the ones that look like preferences and are not. Read them as arguments. Where two
of them would give different advice for the same surface, the disagreement is the
interesting part and it is left in rather than smoothed over.

The two kinds are told apart by which key they declare, and the gate checks them
against different populations, because they are different claims:

- a **Pattern** declares `composes`, naming the Items it arranges, of any kind
- a **Template** declares `arranges`, naming the **Pages** it orders

A Template's subject is a whole screen and a screen is a Page, so a Template naming a
Component has confused itself for a Pattern. The finding says which of the two it
did rather than only reporting a type mismatch, and a document declaring both is
asked which one it is instead of being checked against two populations and getting
neither answer.

A Template is not a copy of a site either. It is the arrangement: which screens
exist, what order they are reached in, and what a reader can get to from where. The
arrangement itself is the published `DocsNavEntry` union rather than a new
vocabulary, which is the shape `DocsShell` already takes and the shape a consumer
site already adapts its own tree into. A site that grew its own navigation vocabulary
would have a second thing to keep in step with the first.
